## 1. Executive Summary

The central contribution of this proposal is **SceneFactory**, a procedural scene construction and multi-world simulation platform built entirely on NVIDIA's Isaac Sim and Isaac Lab stack, designed to train multi-agent autonomous vehicle navigation policies at scale. SceneFactory ingests real-world road topologies derived from the Waymo Open Motion Dataset, procedurally constructs physically realistic USD road environments, and orchestrates the parallel simulation of hundreds of articulated vehicles across dozens of distinct road worlds on a single NVIDIA GPU. The system is operational today. Vehicles trained through its three-stage curriculum can reach designated goals along complex road networks, maintain lane discipline, and respond to surrounding traffic — all learned end-to-end through reinforcement learning on top of NVIDIA PhysX rigid-body dynamics.

What we propose here is the natural next step. SceneFactory was deliberately designed as a general-purpose simulation base, not a one-off training harness. Its procedural scene construction pipeline, its multi-agent environment interface, and its tight integration with NVIDIA's physics and rendering stack make it the right foundation for a series of research directions that would be prohibitively expensive to pursue in the real world or in less capable simulators. Specifically, we propose three extensions that build directly on SceneFactory's existing capabilities: first, the introduction of robotic work zone management as a co-trained agent within the simulation; second, the integration of formal safety metrics into both the training loop and the evaluation pipeline; and third, the extension of our existing weather-aware friction model to support dynamic, spatially varying environmental conditions. Each of these directions depends on SceneFactory's ability to procedurally compose complex scenes, simulate multi-agent physics at high throughput, and maintain a tight GPU-resident training loop — capabilities that exist because of the underlying NVIDIA technology stack.

## 2. SceneFactory: Technical Foundation

### 2.1 What SceneFactory Is

SceneFactory is, at its core, a system for turning real driving scenarios into GPU-parallelized training environments. The problem it solves is deceptively simple to state: given a collection of real-world road geometries and vehicle trajectories, construct a simulator in which many reinforcement learning agents can simultaneously practice driving on those roads, with full rigid-body physics, at a throughput high enough to make policy optimization tractable. In practice, this requires solving a chain of engineering and design problems that span data processing, procedural geometry generation, physics scene setup, multi-agent environment management, and tight integration with a policy optimization loop.

The pipeline begins with the Waymo Open Motion Dataset, which provides thousands of real driving scenarios as TFRecord files. Each scenario contains a set of road polylines — lane centers, road edges, crosswalks, speed bumps — annotated with type codes, widths, and direction vectors, along with agent trajectories that record the positions and headings of vehicles over time. An offline conversion step transforms each scenario into a self-contained JSON manifest that stores the road geometry and a set of start-goal pairs extracted from the agent trajectories. These JSONs are the raw material that SceneFactory consumes.

At scene construction time, SceneFactory reads a batch of these JSON manifests and builds a multi-world simulation stage in NVIDIA Isaac Sim. Each world corresponds to one Waymo scenario and occupies a spatially separated region of the USD stage — for instance, 32 worlds might be arranged in a grid with 200-meter spacing, each containing its own road network. The road geometry is constructed procedurally through what we call the ChocolateBarConstructor, which reads the polyline data from each JSON and generates USD road prims. Two rendering modes are supported: a PointInstancer mode that batches all road segment geometry into a single instancer prim for rendering efficiency, and an explicit-prim mode that creates individual mesh prims per segment for debugging and inspection. Road metadata — the positions, directions, half-widths, and type codes of all road points in each world — is baked into USD custom data attributes on the world root prims so that it can be loaded at runtime in constant time without re-parsing the source JSONs.

Within each world, SceneFactory extracts valid vehicle spawn configurations from the JSON manifest. It filters out degenerate cases — agents that start at their goal, agents whose start or goal lies outside the road bounds — and assigns the remaining start-goal pairs to a fixed number of agent slots per world. If a given scenario provides fewer valid agents than the slot count, the excess slots are marked inactive. The result is a spawn table that maps each (world, agent-slot) pair to a start pose and a goal pose. At training time, this table is used to initialize and reset vehicles without rebuilding the physics scene: a teleport-only reset strategy that repositions vehicles to their spawn poses in a single batched write, avoiding the overhead of tearing down and reconstructing PhysX actors.

The simulation environment itself is implemented as an Isaac Lab DirectMARLEnv — a vectorized multi-agent reinforcement learning environment that runs entirely on the GPU. In the current configuration, SceneFactory supports 32 parallel worlds with up to 8 agents per world, yielding 256 simultaneously active articulated vehicles, all driven by PhysX 5 rigid-body dynamics with full collision detection and contact force reporting. An environment wrapper flattens this multi-agent structure into a single-policy interface: one shared policy network controls all 256 vehicles, receiving an independent observation and producing an independent action for each. This design reflects our belief that the driving task is fundamentally the same for all agents — what differs is the local context each agent sees, not the skill it needs to exercise.

### 2.2 Observations, Actions, and Rewards

The observation space is structured to give each agent a rich but compact view of its local driving context. In the current configuration, each agent receives a 1,929-dimensional observation composed of three semantically distinct groups.

The first group is the ego state, a 7-dimensional vector that encodes the agent's position relative to its goal in the ego frame, its heading error relative to the goal direction, its current speed, and its steering angle. This gives the policy a concise summary of where the agent is, where it needs to go, and what it is currently doing.

The second group is the road geometry context. SceneFactory samples the 350 nearest road points to the agent (using either a KNN query or a sequential road-running sampling strategy) and encodes each as a 5-dimensional feature vector: the point's relative position in the ego frame, a normalized type code indicating whether the point belongs to a lane center, road edge, crosswalk, or other road element, and a direction vector indicating the road's local heading. This produces a 1,750-dimensional road observation. In the late-fusion actor-critic architecture we use, the road branch encodes each point independently through a per-point network and then aggregates across points via max-pooling with validity masking, so the representation is invariant to the number and ordering of nearby road points.

The third group is the neighbor vehicle context. The 24 nearest neighbor vehicles are each encoded as a 7-dimensional feature: relative position, relative velocity, heading difference, and an estimated time-to-collision. The vehicle branch of the late-fusion network mirrors the road branch's structure — a per-entity encoder followed by max-pool aggregation — enabling the agent to reason about a variable number of nearby vehicles without a fixed ordering assumption.

In addition to these three core groups, a 4-dimensional weather context vector is appended: a normalized water film depth and a one-hot encoding of the road surface type (asphalt concrete, stone mastic asphalt, or open-graded friction course). This vector feeds into the ego branch and allows the policy to condition its behavior on road surface conditions.

The action space is deliberately simple: three continuous values per agent, representing throttle, steering, and brake commands. These are decoded into PhysX joint position targets for the front steering joints and drive/brake effort targets for the wheel joints. We chose to keep the action space low-dimensional because the complexity of driving behavior should emerge from the policy's interpretation of its rich observation, not from a high-dimensional action parameterization.

The reward function is a multi-signal composition that we arrived at through extensive iteration. A large sparse bonus is awarded for reaching the goal within a distance threshold. Dense route progress reward encourages steady forward motion along the planned route. A geometric lane-keeping reward provides a small per-step bonus when the agent is near the lane center with good heading alignment. On the penalty side, collisions with other vehicles, crashes, and touching forbidden lane boundaries each incur substantial negative rewards. Continuous penalties discourage off-road driving, idling, and dangerous proximity to other vehicles and road edges through time-to-collision-based terms that scale inversely with TTC. Getting these weights right required considerable experimentation, and the curriculum structure described below was essential to making the full reward signal learnable.

### 2.3 The Training Loop and Curriculum

Policy optimization is performed using Proximal Policy Optimization through the RSL-RL library, with an adaptive learning rate schedule targeting a KL divergence of 0.01, rollouts of 128 steps per environment, 16 mini-batches per update, 5 PPO epochs, and generalized advantage estimation with λ = 0.98 and γ = 0.99. These are well-established choices for Isaac Lab PPO training, and they have worked well with minimal tuning once the reward structure and curriculum were in place.

The policy architecture is a late-fusion actor-critic network with separate weights for the actor and critic. Each observation group is processed by its own encoder branch: the ego state passes through a two-layer MLP with hidden dimensions [64, 64], the road points pass through a per-point MLP with dimensions [96, 96] followed by max-pooling, and the neighbor vehicles pass through an analogous per-entity MLP with max-pooling. The three branch outputs are concatenated and processed by a shared fusion trunk, which feeds into linear heads for the action distribution parameters and the value estimate. All branches use LayerNorm and ReLU activations. The actor and critic maintain separate encoder weights, which we found important for stable training when the reward signal is complex and multi-scale.

Training proceeds through a three-stage curriculum. Stage 1 focuses on navigation fundamentals: goal reaching and route following on a curated set of Waymo scenes, trained from scratch. The reward at this stage emphasizes the goal bonus, route progress, and basic lane-keeping. Stage 2 introduces lane safety, adding road-edge time-to-collision penalties and lane-forbidden penalties, warm-started from a Stage 1 checkpoint. Stage 3 adds the full traffic and weather complexity: multi-agent TTC signals, weather context observations, and friction variation across episodes, warm-started from Stage 2. This progressive structure was essential. Attempting to train with the full reward signal from the start produced policies that failed to learn goal-reaching behavior because the penalty signals dominated the sparse goal bonus before the agent had any chance of experiencing success.

### 2.4 NVIDIA Technologies in SceneFactory

It is worth being explicit about the role each piece of NVIDIA technology plays, because SceneFactory is not merely built on top of these tools in a superficial sense — each one is load-bearing.

NVIDIA Isaac Sim provides the simulation runtime and USD stage management. Every road prim, every vehicle asset, every physics material in SceneFactory is a USD prim managed by Isaac Sim's Omniverse Kit runtime. The procedural scene construction that SceneFactory performs — creating hundreds of road segments, laying out multi-world grids, attaching physics schemas — is all done through the USD and Omniverse APIs that Isaac Sim exposes.

Isaac Lab provides the vectorized environment framework that makes multi-agent training tractable. Its DirectMARLEnv abstraction gives us a multi-agent environment that runs entirely on the GPU, with batched observation computation, batched action application, and batched reset logic. Isaac Lab's Fabric system handles the efficient cloning of physics state across vectorized environments. Without Isaac Lab, we would have had to build a custom vectorized environment wrapper from scratch, which would have cost months of engineering effort.

PhysX 5 is the physics engine that makes SceneFactory's vehicle dynamics credible. Each vehicle is a fully articulated PhysX actor with four wheels, front-wheel steering via position-controlled revolute joints, rear-wheel drive via effort-controlled joints, and contact sensors for collision detection. We performed cross-entropy method system identification to tune PhysX vehicle parameters — tire friction, suspension damping, stiffness — against reference trajectory data, and the resulting dynamics are realistic enough for the trained policies to transfer meaningfully. PhysX also handles the runtime friction material updates that our weather model requires: when SceneFactory sets a new water film thickness for a world, it writes updated friction coefficients directly to the PhysX material properties, and the physics engine picks them up on the next substep.

OpenUSD is the scene description format throughout. Road geometry is represented as UsdGeom.PointInstancer or UsdGeom.Mesh prims. Vehicle assets are articulated USD prims with UsdPhysics schemas. Road metadata is stored as USD custom data. This means that every SceneFactory scene is a standard USD file that can be opened, inspected, and rendered in any Omniverse application — a significant advantage for debugging, visualization, and collaboration.

Finally, the entire training pipeline runs on CUDA through PyTorch. Observations, rewards, policy forward passes, gradient computation, and parameter updates all stay on the GPU as CUDA tensors — there is no bulk data movement between host and device in the inner training loop. The only CPU touches are small scalar reads for conditional branching and logging, which is standard for Isaac Lab environments and does not materially affect throughput. This tight GPU residency is what makes it possible to train 256 vehicles across 32 worlds at thousands of environment steps per second on a single GPU.

## 3. Proposed Research: Building on SceneFactory

The central argument of this proposal is that SceneFactory is not just a training system for one particular driving task — it is a simulation platform that makes a class of research problems newly tractable. The procedural scene construction pipeline can be extended to compose new types of environments. The multi-agent Isaac Lab interface can accommodate new agent types beyond passenger vehicles. The physics-based dynamics and the tight GPU training loop mean that these new problems can be explored at the throughput required for modern reinforcement learning. Below we describe three research directions that we intend to pursue, each of which builds directly on SceneFactory's existing architecture.

### 3.1 Adaptive Robotic Work Zone Management

Highway work zones are among the most dangerous environments for both human and autonomous drivers. The Federal Highway Administration reported over 800 work zone fatalities in 2023 alone, and the number has been trending upward. From a technology standpoint, the problem is twofold. First, autonomous vehicles need to be able to interpret and navigate work zone configurations — temporary lane closures, shifted alignments, taper geometries, reduced speed zones — that differ fundamentally from the nominal road conditions on which most driving policies are trained. Second, the work zone configurations themselves are designed statically: a traffic engineer lays out a taper plan based on MUTCD guidelines, it gets deployed, and it stays fixed regardless of how traffic actually responds. There is a clear opportunity to close this loop, and SceneFactory gives us the simulation infrastructure to do it.

The idea is to extend SceneFactory so that work zone elements — channelizing devices such as cones, drums, and temporary barriers — become first-class objects in the procedurally generated scene, with their own physics (they are PhysX rigid bodies with contact sensors, so a vehicle that strikes a cone will experience a collision event) and their own semantic encoding in the observation space. Taper geometries will be parameterized following MUTCD standards, where the taper length is a function of lane width and posted speed, but with the critical difference that the taper parameters become controllable. Specifically, we will represent the taper as a trapezoidal region defined by four continuous parameters: the lateral offset angles at the upstream and downstream ends and the longitudinal extents of the upstream and downstream transitions. These four numbers define where the lane closure begins, how sharply traffic is merged, and how much downstream buffer is provided before the work activity zone.

The novel element of this research is the introduction of a second learned agent: the work zone controller. In SceneFactory's current architecture, the multi-agent environment hosts multiple vehicle agents that share a single driving policy. We will extend this to host a heterogeneous agent population: vehicle agents that drive through the work zone, and a work zone controller agent that observes the upstream traffic and adjusts the taper geometry in response. The work zone controller's observation will consist of aggregated traffic state features — mean speed, flow rate, queue length, and density in the approach segment — derived from a simulated traffic sensor. We plan to model this sensor using Isaac Sim's RTX ray-tracing pipeline to simulate a LiDAR-like detection fan covering the upstream approach, with per-vehicle returns processed through a recurrent traffic state estimator that maintains a short temporal history. The controller's action space is the four-dimensional taper parameterization described above, plus a scalar speed advisory that is communicated to the vehicle agents as part of their observation.

The intuition behind this formulation is that a work zone's effectiveness depends on how well its geometry matches the traffic it is trying to manage. A taper that is too short for the prevailing speed and density will produce abrupt merges and elevated collision risk. A taper that is too long wastes road capacity and may cause confusion if drivers cannot discern where the merge is supposed to happen. The optimal taper shape depends on the traffic conditions, and traffic conditions depend on the taper shape — this is a coupled system, and solving it requires training both sides simultaneously.

The training protocol has three phases. In the first phase, we train vehicle policies on static work zone configurations — fixed tapers, fixed speed advisories — using SceneFactory's existing curriculum as a warm start. This gives us a population of vehicles that can navigate work zones competently but cannot adapt to changes in the work zone layout. In the second phase, we freeze the vehicle policy and train the work zone controller to maximize a composite objective that balances traffic throughput (vehicles per hour through the work zone), safety metrics (the distribution of time-to-collision values and the frequency of hard braking events in the taper region), and queue management (minimizing the length and duration of upstream queuing). In the third phase, we release both policies for co-training, alternating optimization with decreasing freeze intervals, with the goal of converging to a configuration where vehicles respond fluently to the controller's taper adjustments and the controller has learned to shape the taper in a way that accounts for how vehicles actually behave.

This co-training is possible because SceneFactory already supports multi-agent training through Isaac Lab's DirectMARLEnv interface. The extension to heterogeneous agent types — adding a work zone controller agent alongside the vehicle agents — is architecturally straightforward within this framework. What makes it a research contribution is not the engineering of the multi-agent interface but the formulation of the problem itself and the investigation of whether co-trained policies can outperform static MUTCD-standard configurations on safety and throughput metrics.

The work zone controller will also be equipped with a realistic sensor model. Rather than giving the controller direct access to the simulator's ground-truth traffic state, we will simulate a sensor pipeline: a 2D detection fan covering the upstream approach with a 200-meter range and 120-degree field of view, returning per-vehicle range, bearing, and radial velocity. These returns will be processed through a GRU-based traffic state estimator that outputs the flow, density, and queue features the controller actually uses for decision-making. This sensor-to-action pipeline, trainable end-to-end within SceneFactory, ensures that the controller's learned behavior is grounded in what a real robotic work zone device could plausibly observe, not in privileged simulator information.

### 3.2 Safety Metric Integration

A trained driving policy is only as useful as the confidence one can place in its safety properties. SceneFactory's current reward function includes time-to-collision penalties, which provide a first approximation to safety-aware training, but a serious treatment of safety requires both a richer set of metrics and a more principled integration into the training and evaluation pipeline.

We plan to implement a comprehensive safety metric suite within SceneFactory, operating at two levels. At the evaluation level, every trained policy will be assessed against a standardized scorecard that includes time-to-collision distributions across all agent-timesteps, post-encroachment time distributions at conflict points such as merges and lane changes, deceleration rate to avoid crash (DRAC) exceedance rates, and collision rates broken down by type. Post-encroachment time is particularly important for work zone scenarios, where vehicles merge through a common spatial region in sequence: the PET measures how much temporal separation exists between consecutive vehicles occupying the same conflict zone, and low PET values indicate near-miss events that would not be captured by collision rate alone. These metrics will be computed over thousands of evaluation episodes using GPU-parallel PyTorch tensor operations on CUDA, producing statistically meaningful assessments that go well beyond the anecdotal evaluation common in the RL for driving literature.

At the training level, we intend to integrate NVIDIA's Responsibility-Sensitive Safety framework as a differentiable constraint layer on the policy output. The concept is that at each timestep, the policy proposes an action, the RSS module evaluates whether that action would violate the safe following distance, safe lateral distance, or right-of-way rules defined by the RSS model, and if it would, the action is replaced by the minimal-intervention proper response — typically, a comfortable deceleration that restores safe separation. The implementation challenge is making this constraint compatible with gradient-based policy optimization. We plan to use a straight-through estimator that passes gradients through the constraint during backpropagation, allowing the policy to learn to avoid triggering the safety filter rather than relying on it as a permanent crutch. Over the course of training, we expect the RSS violation rate to decrease as the policy internalizes the safety constraints, and the final policy should exhibit a natural margin of safety that does not depend on the presence of the filter at deployment time.

We also plan to extend the time-to-collision computation from its current constant-velocity formulation to a modified TTC that accounts for relative acceleration, which requires solving a quadratic rather than a linear equation but provides substantially more accurate collision risk estimates in scenarios where vehicles are actively accelerating or decelerating. This is particularly important in work zone approach zones, where vehicles transition from highway speed to a reduced advisory speed and the constant-velocity TTC assumption systematically overestimates collision risk during deceleration phases.

The safety metric suite is not a standalone contribution — it is the evaluation backbone for the work zone research. When we claim that a co-trained work zone controller outperforms a static MUTCD taper on safety grounds, the claim needs to be backed by rigorous, multi-dimensional safety assessment, not just a single collision rate number. The metric suite provides that rigor.

### 3.3 Weather-Aware Friction Dynamics

SceneFactory already includes a weather-aware friction model based on the Zhao et al. (2024) tire-pavement friction framework. This model computes an effective friction coefficient as a function of water film thickness and road surface type, and it is integrated into SceneFactory at two points: it sets PhysX material friction properties per world at simulation time, and it provides a weather context vector in the policy's observation space. The current implementation treats friction as static within each episode — a water film thickness is sampled at episode start and held constant throughout.

We propose two extensions that significantly increase the environmental realism and the difficulty of the driving task.

The first extension is dynamic weather transitions. Instead of a fixed friction per episode, the water film thickness will evolve over time according to a stochastic process that models precipitation onset, intensification, and cessation. PhysX material properties will be updated at each simulation substep through Isaac Lab's EventTerm system, so the vehicle experiences changing road conditions in real time within a single episode. The interesting training question here is whether the policy can learn to detect friction changes through proprioceptive cues — subtle shifts in wheel slip ratio, lateral acceleration variance, or yaw rate response to steering inputs — without being given explicit weather observations. We plan to experiment with both observation modes: one in which the weather context vector is provided (the straightforward case) and one in which it is withheld, which more closely resembles reality, where a vehicle must infer road conditions from its own dynamics. The latter setting is substantially harder and more interesting, and success there would represent a meaningful step toward policies that are robust to unobserved environmental variation.

The second extension is spatially varying friction within a single world. Different road segments may have different surface types and different water accumulation patterns — puddles in low spots, ice on bridge decks, gravel on construction zone shoulders. SceneFactory's road construction pipeline already tags each road segment with a type code; extending this to support per-segment friction overrides is a natural generalization that introduces a spatial dimension to the friction challenge. A policy must learn to adjust its behavior not just over time as weather changes but over space as it transitions between surface types, which is a more demanding form of environmental adaptation.

These weather extensions connect directly to the work zone research. A work zone controller that receives friction estimates as part of its observation can adjust its taper geometry and speed advisories in response to deteriorating road conditions. MUTCD guidelines already prescribe longer tapers under reduced-friction conditions; a learned controller can go further, discovering taper configurations that are empirically optimal for specific friction levels and traffic densities, rather than relying on the conservative closed-form formulas in the manual. The combination of dynamic friction, multi-agent traffic, and adaptive work zone control produces a training scenario of genuine complexity — the kind of scenario where learning-based approaches have the best chance of outperforming rule-based design, and the kind of scenario that is only feasible to explore at scale in a simulator like SceneFactory.

## 4. Why NVIDIA

We want to be direct about why this proposal is specifically suited for NVIDIA support. The research program described here is not GPU-accelerated in a generic sense — it depends on capabilities that are specific to the NVIDIA simulation and compute ecosystem, and it would not be feasible on alternative platforms without years of additional infrastructure development.

Isaac Sim is the simulation runtime that makes procedural scene construction at this scale possible. Every road surface, every articulated vehicle, every contact event in SceneFactory is managed by Isaac Sim's Omniverse Kit runtime. The ability to programmatically construct multi-world USD stages with thousands of prims and simulate them at interactive rates is not something we could replicate with MuJoCo, PyBullet, or any other open-source physics engine — not because those engines lack physics quality, but because they lack the scene management, USD pipeline, and vectorized simulation infrastructure that Isaac Sim provides.

Isaac Lab is what makes multi-agent RL training at this throughput tractable. Its DirectMARLEnv abstraction and Fabric-based state cloning are the specific pieces of infrastructure that allow us to run 256 articulated vehicles across 32 worlds in a single training loop without per-environment overhead eating the throughput gains from GPU parallelism. When we propose extending SceneFactory to host heterogeneous agent types for the work zone research, we are extending Isaac Lab's existing multi-agent interface — the scaffolding is already there.

PhysX 5 is used not as a black-box collision checker but as a credible vehicle dynamics engine. Our vehicles are fully articulated PhysX actors with joint-level control, and we have performed formal system identification to calibrate their dynamics against reference data. The weather-aware friction model depends on PhysX's ability to accept dynamic material property changes during simulation — a capability that not all physics engines support at runtime. The work zone devices we propose to add are PhysX rigid bodies with contact sensors. The fidelity of our trained policies is directly tied to the fidelity of PhysX.

The proposed RSS constraint layer integration is a direct contribution to NVIDIA's Responsibility-Sensitive Safety ecosystem. If successful, it demonstrates that RSS can function not just as a runtime safety filter but as a training signal that shapes policy behavior, which would be a novel and practically useful result.

Finally, OpenUSD as the scene format means that everything SceneFactory produces — road environments, vehicle assets, training scenes — is interoperable with the broader Omniverse ecosystem and with NVIDIA DRIVE Sim. This is not a theoretical benefit; it means that a scene constructed by SceneFactory can be opened in Omniverse for visualization, shared with collaborators, or loaded into DRIVE Sim for further validation, without format conversion or custom tooling.

## 5. Compute Requirements

The current SceneFactory configuration runs on a single NVIDIA A100 80GB GPU and achieves several thousand environment steps per second with 256 simultaneous vehicles. The proposed extensions will increase per-world complexity through the addition of work zone devices, dynamic friction updates, and sensor simulation for the work zone controller, and may require scaling to 64 or more worlds for the co-training experiments. We estimate that the core training runs will require two A100 80GB or H100 GPUs, with an additional RTX A6000 for ray-traced sensor simulation and rendering. A 32-core CPU workstation with 256 GB of system memory will support the offline USD stage construction and data preprocessing. Storage requirements are approximately 2 TB for the Waymo dataset, checkpoints, and logs.

The software stack is NVIDIA Isaac Sim 4.x, Isaac Lab 2.x, PhysX 5.x, Warp 1.x, CUDA 12.x, and PyTorch 2.x, with RSL-RL for policy optimization.

The first three months will focus on work zone scene augmentation within SceneFactory: extending the procedural scene construction pipeline to generate channelizing devices, taper geometries, and temporary signage as USD prims with PhysX physics properties, and training vehicle policies on static work zone configurations using the existing curriculum as a warm start. Months seven through nine will tackle the full co-training loop — releasing both vehicle and controller policies for alternating optimization — and the RSS constraint layer integration. The weather extensions will be developed in parallel, since they are architecturally independent of the co-training work.

The final three months will focus on comprehensive evaluation, running the full safety scorecard across all trained policy variants under nominal, work zone, and adverse weather conditions, followed by paper preparation and an open-source release of the SceneFactory extensions as a contribution to the Isaac Lab ecosystem. We are targeting CoRL 2027, ICRA 2027, and IEEE IV 2027 as publication venues, with NVIDIA co-authorship where appropriate.

## 8. Discussion: SceneFactory as a Source of Physically Grounded Video Generation via NVIDIA Cosmos

Beyond the policy training and safety evaluation tasks described above, SceneFactory opens a further downstream opportunity that aligns closely with NVIDIA's investment in generative world models: using SceneFactory's rendered rollouts as reference guidance for photorealistic video generation through NVIDIA Cosmos.

The key observation is that SceneFactory produces something that most driving video datasets cannot: temporally extended sequences of multi-agent driving behavior that are both physically grounded and behaviorally coherent. When a trained policy drives 256 vehicles through 32 distinct road worlds, each episode produces a trajectory that respects PhysX rigid-body dynamics, obeys the lane geometry of a real Waymo road network, and reflects the emergent interactions between co-trained agents — vehicles yielding, merging, braking for each other, navigating work zones. These trajectories are not scripted or replayed from logs; they are the product of a learned policy interacting with a physics engine, which means they exhibit the kind of causal consistency that generative models struggle to produce on their own. A vehicle slows down because there is a cone ahead, not because a diffusion model hallucinated plausible deceleration.

Isaac Sim can render these rollouts from arbitrary camera viewpoints at each simulation step, producing sequences of images or video clips that capture the scene from ego-vehicle perspectives, bird's-eye views, or roadside observation angles. The rendered output is not photorealistic — Isaac Sim's Omniverse rendering is high-quality but unmistakably synthetic — and this is precisely where NVIDIA Cosmos enters the picture. Cosmos is NVIDIA's family of world foundation models designed for physical AI, including video-to-video transfer capabilities that can take a synthetic reference video with correct geometry, motion, and physical behavior, and re-render it into a photorealistic output that preserves the spatial layout, vehicle trajectories, and temporal dynamics of the input while replacing the synthetic textures and lighting with learned photorealistic appearance.

The combination is powerful because it addresses a fundamental tension in autonomous driving data generation. Purely generative approaches (text-to-video, image-to-video) can produce photorealistic frames but cannot guarantee that the depicted driving behavior is physically plausible — vehicles may clip through each other, accelerate impossibly, or ignore road geometry in ways that are visually subtle but dynamically wrong. Purely simulation-based approaches produce physically correct behavior but look synthetic, which limits their utility for training perception models that must operate on real camera imagery. SceneFactory plus Cosmos offers a way to get both: the physical correctness comes from PhysX dynamics and the learned driving policies, and the visual realism comes from Cosmos's generative transfer.

Concretely, we envision a pipeline in which SceneFactory generates large libraries of driving scenarios — including the work zone configurations, weather conditions, and multi-agent interactions described in this proposal — and renders each scenario from multiple viewpoints at simulation frame rate. These rendered sequences, along with their associated metadata (camera extrinsics, vehicle bounding boxes, road geometry, semantic labels), are then fed to Cosmos as conditioning inputs for photorealistic video synthesis. The resulting videos would be physically realistic because the underlying trajectories come from a calibrated physics engine, behaviorally realistic because the driving decisions come from trained multi-agent policies, and visually realistic because the appearance comes from Cosmos's learned photorealistic transfer. Such a dataset would be valuable for training and validating perception systems, for generating rare or dangerous scenarios (work zone near-misses, wet-road loss-of-control events) that are difficult to collect in the real world, and for producing synthetic training data that complements real-world driving logs.

This is not a deliverable of the current proposal — it is a natural next phase that becomes possible once SceneFactory's scene diversity and behavioral richness reach the level we are targeting. We raise it here because it illustrates SceneFactory's role as a long-term infrastructure investment rather than a single-use research tool, and because the SceneFactory-to-Cosmos pipeline would be a compelling demonstration of how NVIDIA's simulation stack (Isaac Sim, PhysX, USD) and generative AI stack (Cosmos) can be composed to produce something neither could achieve alone.
